### Response: Inclusion of benchmark training data and fairness of comparisons

We thank the reviewer for raising this concern. We agree that training-data composition is important when interpreting benchmark results and comparing models trained under different conditions. However, we believe that the inclusion of benchmark training examples should be considered in the context of established training practices for diffusion language models, and that an important distinction should be made between using benchmark training splits and contaminating held-out evaluation data.

**First, BYOD uses a broad, general-purpose training mixture rather than benchmark-specific fine-tuning.** Our diffusion adaptation dataset consists of one million examples, comprising 45% general instruction following, 18% multiple-choice reasoning, 18% mathematics, and 19% code generation. Approximately 19% of this mixture is explicitly derived from publicly available benchmark training splits, primarily MMLU (13.14%) and HellaSwag (4.50%), with smaller contributions from ARC-Easy, GSM8K, and MBPP. The remaining approximately 81% comes from broader instruction, reasoning, mathematics, and coding sources.

All four model families are trained using the same mixture and training procedure, without separate fine-tuning for individual benchmarks. Moreover, we exclusively use training splits where applicable, deduplicate normalized prompts, and exclude exact matches with held-out evaluation prompts. Although these measures cannot exclude all potential indirect contamination, they distinguish our methodology from training on evaluation examples.

**Second, the use of benchmark-related training data is not unique to BYOD and is also present in prominent diffusion language model research.**

For example, Dream 7B (Ye et al., 2025) performs supervised fine-tuning using Tulu 3 and SmolLM2 instruction mixtures. Tulu 3 explicitly contains mathematical and reasoning-oriented datasets, including Persona GSM, Persona MATH, and NuminaMath-TIR. Notably, Tulu 3 also constitutes 15.75% of BYOD's training mixture, while OpenCoder, another source used in Dream's training pipeline, accounts for approximately 18.94%. Thus, both approaches use overlapping upstream data sources containing instruction, mathematical reasoning, and coding examples closely related to downstream evaluation tasks. Although this does not establish identical exposure to specific benchmark training examples, it demonstrates that such task-related training data are not unique to our approach.

More directly, *d1: Scaling Reasoning in Diffusion Large Language Models via Reinforcement Learning* explicitly uses benchmark-specific mathematical reasoning data for post-training and evaluates on corresponding held-out benchmark examples. While d1 investigates task-specific reasoning improvements rather than general-purpose AR-to-diffusion conversion, it illustrates that using benchmark training data for diffusion model adaptation is an established experimental approach and is not inherently considered test-set contamination.

**Third, complete training-data provenance is not consistently available for other foundational diffusion language models.** For example, LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025), published at NeurIPS, reports large-scale pretraining on 2.3 trillion tokens and supervised fine-tuning on 4.5 million instruction-response pairs, but does not release its complete training datasets. Consequently, independent verification of whether specific benchmark training examples were encountered is not possible. We do not suggest that LLaDA necessarily used such examples; rather, the absence of explicit reporting should not be interpreted as evidence of their exclusion.

In contrast, BYOD explicitly documents the composition and relative proportions of its training sources, the use of training splits, and its filtering procedures, thereby enabling more detailed scrutiny of potential benchmark exposure.

**Finally, we acknowledge that differences in training-data composition may influence absolute benchmark performance.** In particular, we do not claim that including benchmark training examples has no effect or that all compared diffusion models have equivalent training exposure. However, our principal contribution is not benchmark-specific optimization or state-of-the-art accuracy, but demonstrating that pretrained autoregressive language models can be converted into functional diffusion language models across multiple architectural families using a computationally efficient, parameter-efficient adaptation procedure.

We therefore agree that training-data differences should be considered when interpreting performance comparisons, but believe that BYOD's training methodology is consistent with practices found elsewhere in diffusion language model research and does not undermine its central methodological contribution. We would also like to emphasize the importance of making training and finetuning datasets public, specifically for grounded comparisons and to encourage academic discourse such as this.&#x20;
