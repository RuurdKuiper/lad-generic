\begin{abstract}
Diffusion language models (DLMs) permit parallel token updates and flexible generation orders, but leading systems rely on costly pretraining or continued full-model training. We ask whether diffusion generation can instead be added to existing instruction-tuned autoregressive models using a single parameter-efficient conversion recipe. Build Your Own DLM (BYOD) replaces causal attention with bidirectional attention and trains only query/value LoRA adapters under a masked denoising objective, leaving 94.2–95.9\% of parameters frozen. Without architecture-specific tuning, the same recipe converts Gemma 2 9B, Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, and Ministral 8B, with each 25,000-update conversion processing at most 102.4M token positions on a single GPU. All four converted models learn to generate coherent responses from fully masked sequences. On a preliminary nine-task evaluation, the strongest converted model reaches 50.7\% average accuracy, compared with 54.0\% for LLaDA 8B Instruct in the same evaluation harness. Continued training does not improve aggregate downstream accuracy, but improves generation quality when the number of denoising network function evaluations (NFE) is smaller than the output length. Together, these results provide a practical route for researchers to build diffusion language models from existing autoregressive checkpoints using only parameter-efficient adaptation and single-GPU compute.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Autoregressive (AR) language models generate a sequence from left to right. This method has proven extremely effective due to its simple learning target and effective scaling capabilities. However, it also has trade-offs: it fixes the generation order, limits the model to unidirectional attention, and predicts only one new token at a time. Diffusion language models (DLMs) instead begin with a corrupted sequence and can iteratively reconstruct multiple positions at a time using bidirectional context \citep{austin2021structured,sahoo2024simple,shi2024simplified}. Their number of function evaluations (NFE) can be chosen independently of output length, and early low-probability predictions can be revised. However, training or adapting models for diffusive generation has so far been computationally intensive and therefore expensive.

Our aim is to provide a practical alternative to training a separate diffusion foundation model for every model architecture family, reusing knowledge embedded in existing AR models reduces the data, compute, and financial barriers to experimentation. This emphasis follows calls to treat computational efficiency and access as research outcomes alongside accuracy \citep{strubell2019energy}. We also estimate the potential sustainability impact, while recognizing that compute volume alone is not a carbon measurement: emissions depend strongly on hardware, datacenter efficiency, location, and energy supply \citep{patterson2021carbon}. We report bounded incremental training scale and an explicitly assumption-based operational-emissions scenario, while avoiding claims about measured emissions.

We study this question with \method{}, a generalizable conversion and evaluation pipeline for four instruction-tuned decoder families: Gemma~2~9B, Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen2.5~7B, and Ministral~8B. We selected the latest text-only instruction checkpoint from each family with a comparable parameter count. Multimodal variants are not used to avoid modality-specific training as an additional confounder. Every backbone receives the same bidirectional attention intervention, LoRA target modules, and masked-diffusion objective. This paired design separates conversion quality from the strength of the starting checkpoint. We directly compare our models against LLaDA~8B Instruct because its standard masked-diffusion objective, instruction-tuning setup, and overlapping benchmark suite make it the cleanest external control. We additionally report published DiffuLLaMA, Dream-Instruct, iLLaDA-Instruct, and Efficient-DLM values, but keep them protocol-separated: their additional attention, masking, scheduling, data, and inference techniques would confound a controlled comparison.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a single, easily reproducible, architecture-agnostic recipe that converts four instruction-tuned AR model families to masked diffusion using only LoRA parameter updates on a single Google Colab GPU;
    \item a paired evaluation of each diffusion model and its frozen AR parent across general knowledge, reasoning, mathematics, and code benchmarks;
    \item a controlled analysis of base-model choice, continued training, denoising-step count, validation loss, and externally scored generation perplexity; and
    \item a transparent comparison between results produced by our common harness and the published values reported with the LLaDA evaluation protocol.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

Discrete diffusion models corrupt and reconstruct categorical sequences rather than adding Gaussian noise. D3PM formalized categorical corruption with transition matrices, including an absorbing process in which tokens transition to a dedicated mask state \citep{austin2021structured}. MDLM and contemporaneous work showed that this masked formulation admits a simple noise-weighted cross-entropy objective, while RADD reparameterized the predictor in terms of conditional distributions over clean tokens \citep{sahoo2024simple,shi2024simplified,ou2024absorbing}. These approaches form the basis of masked diffusion language models (MDLMs). Uniform (or multinomial) diffusion, which instead corrupts tokens with samples from the ordinary vocabulary, is another option \citep{hoogeboom2021multinomial}; we use the simpler absorbing-mask denoising objective employed by LLaDA.

The authors of LLaDA were the first to show that masked diffusion can scale to an 8B-parameter general-purpose language model \citep{nie2025llada}. Although its architecture follows Llama~3.1~8B, LLaDA was pre-trained from scratch using 2.3T tokens and 130,000 GPU-hours. AR-to-diffusion conversion instead amortizes knowledge already acquired by an AR checkpoint. DiffuLLaMA established this route through 65B tokens of full-model continued training at 7B scale \citep{gong2025diffullama}. Dream adopts DiffuLLaMA's shifted-prediction initialization, continues Qwen2.5 for 580B tokens, and adds context-adaptive token-level noise rescheduling \citep{ye2025dream}. Efficient-DLM combines 500B tokens of continued training with blockwise attention and position-dependent masking \citep{fu2025efficientdlm}, whereas iLLaDA returns to from-scratch diffusion pretraining at substantially larger scale \citep{nie2026illada}. Diffusion conversion has also been demonstrated at larger sparse scales by RND1 (30B parameters), LLaDA2.0 (16B and 100B), and DiffusionGemma (25.2B, using uniform diffusion) \citep{chandrasegaran2025rnd1,bie2025llada20,diffusiongemma2026}. 

Hybrid and blockwise models offer a different compromise between bidirectionality and deployability. Block Diffusion is autoregressive across blocks but diffusive within each block, enabling variable-length generation and inter-block KV caching \citep{arriola2025block}. Fast-dLLM v2 converts Qwen2.5 models using full-parameter fine-tuning with approximately 1B tokens and combines blockwise complementary masking with hierarchical caching \citep{wu2025fastdllmv2}. Sequential Diffusion Language Models (SDLM) instead learn next-sequence prediction and dynamically finalize variable-length subsequences, reporting conversion with 2.3 billion tokens \citep{liu2025sdlm}. These systems retain more left-to-right structure than our fully bidirectional answer block, making them especially relevant to speed, caching, and arbitrary-length generation but not suited as direct controls for our intervention.

LAD first showed that LoRA can induce diffusion generation, using a hybrid objective that combines masking with token swaps, duplications, and span shifts \citep{kuiper2025lad,hu2022lora}. \method{} instead tests whether a simpler IID absorbing-mask objective can transfer reliably across model families. Other LoRA-based diffusion work addresses different goals: Uno learns diffusion-draft adapters while retaining the AR model as verifier, the dLLM framework uses LoRA to fine-tune already-diffusive checkpoints rather than for its Tiny-A2D conversion, and NaRA adapts existing diffusion models with noise-conditioned LoRA \citep{sahoo2026uno,zhou2026dllm,wang2026nara}. Thus, their LoRA components augment already diffusively generating models, rather than converting AR model families into standalone diffusion models.

For AR-to-diffusion conversion methods, these budgets exclude the substantial earlier cost of training the AR parent; for from-scratch methods they include diffusion pretraining. \section{Method}

\subsection{Masked-diffusion training objective}

The conversion objective teaches the adapted model to recover clean answer tokens from randomly masked versions of the same response while conditioning on an uncorrupted instruction. Equation~\ref{eq:objective} formalizes this reconstruction loss and its correction for the sampled noise level; the following subsections describe the bidirectional attention intervention and the restricted set of parameters used to optimize it.

Let $p$ be an observed instruction prompt and $x_0=(x_0^1,\ldots,x_0^L)$ its clean response region, where $L$ is the response-region length and $x_0^i$ is its token at position $i$. For each example, we sample
$t\sim\mathcal{U}[10^{-3},1]$ and independently replace each eligible response token with a single tokenizer-native mask representation with probability $t$. Denote the resulting sequence by $x_t$ and the sampled mask positions by $M_t$. The model is trained with same-position cross-entropy,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) =
    -\mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,x_t}
    \left[
    \frac{1}{t|E|}\sum_{i\in M_t}
    \log p_{\phi,\theta}(x_0^i\mid p,x_t)
    \right],
    \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the set of eligible answer-and-padding positions, $|E|$ is its cardinality, $M_t\subseteq E$ is the subset masked at noise level $t$, and $p_{\phi,\theta}$ is the model's conditional token distribution. The expectation is over training examples, sampled noise levels, and their resulting corruptions; $\phi$ denotes the frozen parent parameters and $\theta$ the LoRA parameters. Prompt and chat-template tokens are always visible and never enter the loss. Training corruption is resampled online; validation corruption is deterministic from the example index and seed.

The clean terminating EOS is included as a genuine answer token. To teach the fixed-width generator to emit termination beyond the answer, dynamic batch padding is represented by repeated EOS tokens; these positions are also eligible for masking and loss. Thus padding is part of the learned denoising target rather than conventional ignored padding.

\subsection{Bidirectional conversion and trainable parameters}

We replace the causal mask with an all-zero four-dimensional additive attention mask, so every prompt, answer, and EOS-padding position can attend to every position. The architecture and output vocabulary remain unchanged. This intervention makes right-hand response evidence available to masked positions; Section~\ref{sec:bidirectional-infilling} tests whether the converted models use that access in practice. We attach LoRA adapters to every self-attention query and value projection, with rank $r=1024$, scaling $\alpha=1024$, and dropout zero. This high rank reflects the scale of the intervention: the adapters must learn a new conditional denoising process rather than a narrow downstream task. In LAD's rank study, increasing rank from 512 to 1024 at 3B improved every reported benchmark and perplexity, whereas increasing from 1024 to 2048 at 8B gave no consistent further gain; our preliminary rank experiments showed the same pattern. We therefore chose 1024 as a compromise between conversion capacity and training footprint \citep{kuiper2025lad}. Backbone weights, token embeddings, the language-model head, biases, and normalization layers are frozen. Each model reuses an existing single-token string from its tokenizer (\texttt{MASK} for Gemma, Llama, and Qwen; \texttt{<?>} for Ministral), avoiding vocabulary expansion.

After training, we tested whether folding the LoRA layers into the parent projection would change the performance of the model, defined as: $W_{\mathrm{merged}}=W+(\alpha/r)BA$, where $W$ is the frozen base projection, $A$ and $B$ are the trained low-rank adapter matrices, $r$ is their rank, and $\alpha$ is the LoRA scaling parameter. The resulting standalone checkpoint retains bidirectional inference but has the base architecture's parameter count: Llama decreases from 8.466B adapter-loaded parameters to 8.030B merged parameters.

\subsection{Training data}

All main models use the same training dataset and seed-selected ordering. Examples are retokenized with each parent model's native chat template and tokenizer. Only the current answer region is corrupted. The dataset builder groups sources into general instruction following, multiple-choice reasoning, mathematics, and code, deduplicates normalized prompts, and blocks exact normalized matches with represented benchmark validation/test prompts.

The four primary models see the same dataset prefix. The continued Llama condition initializes from the primary adapter and uses the next non-overlapping segment of the stable shuffle. It resamples corruption masks and restarts AdamW and its learning-rate schedule; it is therefore continued adapter training, not a restoration of optimizer state. Exact stage sizes are consolidated in Appendix~\ref{app:training-settings}.

\subsection{Optimization}

We use AdamW with peak learning rate $10^{-5}$ and train with BF16 master weights plus Transformer Engine HYBRID FP8 execution. All reported conversions ran on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition in Google Colab, without multi-GPU or multi-node parallelism; the code, memory strategy, and adapter scope were explicitly designed around this constraint. Appendix~\ref{app:training-settings} gives the complete optimization, checkpointing, validation, and reproducibility configuration.

\subsection{Diffusion generation}

Generation begins from a fully masked answer block. At each reverse step the model predicts every masked position, after which the least-confident positions are retained for reconsideration according to the LLaDA fixed-transfer schedule; newly accepted positions become permanent context. The primary factual and code benchmarks use greedy token selection and low-confidence remasking. Open-ended evaluation instead samples at temperature 0.7 and delays acceptance of EOS/EOT predictions. Complete task-specific diffusion and AR budgets are reported separately from training details in Appendix~\ref{app:inference-settings}.

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Paired models and baselines}
For Gemma, Llama, Qwen, and Ministral we compare the diffusion adapter with greedy generation from its unchanged AR parent. LLaDA~8B Instruct is evaluated as an external diffusion baseline. We report two explicitly separated LLaDA comparisons: (i) LLaDA run through our prompts, sampling code, subsets, and scorers; and (ii) values transcribed from the LLaDA paper/evaluation suite. The latter are contextual references rather than directly matched measurements.

\subsection{Benchmarks and scoring}

Our benchmark set contains the validation/test sets of MMLU, MMLU-Pro, HellaSwag, ARC-Challenge, GSM8K, MATH-500, GPQA-Main, HumanEval, and sanitized MBPP \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu,wang2024mmlupro,zellers2019hellaswag,clark2018arc,cobbe2021gsm8k,hendrycks2021math,rein2023gpqa,chen2021codex,austin2021mbpp}. We score multiple-choice tasks by option label, GSM8K by its final number, MATH by its normalized final boxed expression, and code tasks by whether the generated program passes the supplied tests in an isolated subprocess.

We evaluate functional use of bidirectional context separately from these capability benchmarks with a deterministic in-place infilling diagnostic (Appendix~\ref{app:bidirectional-infilling}). Each example places a fully masked target span between an answer prefix and a suffix that contains the decisive target evidence. All target tokens are masked simultaneously, so the model cannot recover later target tokens from earlier target tokens. We compare the converted model's one-pass same-position predictions with the suffix present against a prefix-only pass of the same model. For unmerged adapters, we additionally score the untouched causal parent by conventional teacher forcing; this control is deliberately favorable to the AR model but cannot access the right-hand evidence. The diagnostic tests whether the attention intervention is functionally used, rather than measuring broad natural-language reasoning.

For 100 fixed open-ended prompts, Microsoft Phi-4 14B scores each generated answer autoregressively. Our primary aggregate is token-weighted perplexity, $\exp(\sum_j \mathrm{NLL}_j/\sum_j N_j)$, so each scored token contributes equally. Generative perplexity does not penalize loss of sample diversity \citep{turok2026duel}. We therefore also compute sliding model-token Distinct-1/2/3 after excluding special tokens. Higher Distinct-$n$ means a larger fraction of unique $n$-grams.

\subsection{Base-model dependence and LLaDA comparison}

All four converted families produce usable diffusion generations, but the resulting quality is not architecture invariant. Gemma currently has the strongest complete BYOD diffusion macro average, while Qwen and Llama are similar on average but differ by task. The incomplete Ministral row already illustrates another important point: its strong multiple-choice and GSM8K scores do not establish code or MATH capability until those evaluations finish. \xxx{Add confidence intervals and formal paired comparisons once the evaluation size is expanded.}

LLaDA scores 54.0\% on the nine-task unweighted average in our harness, compared with 54.5\% from its published values. Agreement varies by task and should not be interpreted as an exact reproduction because prompts, subsets, generation lengths, answer extraction, and code execution may differ. We therefore present our common-harness comparison as primary and Appendix~\ref{app:published-results} only as external calibration. We chose LLaDA for direct external testing because it uses the uncomplicated response-masking objective closest to ours and reports the largest overlap with our task suite. Dream, iLLaDA, and Efficient-DLM add different data scales, noise weighting, SFT formats, scoring rules, attention patterns, or positional masking. Importing those techniques into the primary experiment would prevent us from attributing outcomes to parameter-efficient conversion itself.

\section{Discussion}

The central empirical finding is qualitative but robust across the selected runs: high-rank attention-only LoRA is sufficient to turn four independently developed causal instruction models into generators that reconstruct answers from a fully masked block. This supports the view that much of the knowledge needed for diffusion generation is already present in AR representations and vocabulary heads. Because each conversion fits on one Google Colab GPU rather than requiring a dedicated multi-accelerator cluster, the method also lowers the infrastructure threshold for reproducing, modifying, and teaching large-scale diffusion-language-model conversion. A capable paid cloud GPU is still required, so this is broader access rather than zero-cost access.

The merge result also shows that LoRA's parameter-count overhead is not permanent: the deployed Llama returns from 8.466B to 8.030B parameters without a material open-generation degradation in this small matched comparison (Appendix~\ref{app:merge}). Algebraically folding $(\alpha/r)BA$ into $W$ removes both the stored adapter branch and its extra low-rank inference matrix multiplications. Consequently, using hundreds of millions of high-rank LoRA parameters during conversion need not make the final generator larger or more expensive per network evaluation than the base architecture. This is not model compression---the full base-sized checkpoint remains---and diffusion decoding can still require many network evaluations; more prompts, deterministic parity checks, and backbones are needed to bound small numerical or sampling differences after merging.

Conversion is not free, however. First, 323--573M trainable parameters are parameter-efficient relative to full-model training but substantially larger than conventional low-rank task adapters. Second, our secondary perplexity remains worse than each AR parent and than LLaDA at 64 steps. Third, additional Llama training does not improve the provisional downstream benchmark aggregate, but its largest fluency gains occur when $\mathrm{NFE}<L$. This suggests a more specific benefit: additional denoising training may teach the model to resolve multiple mutually dependent positions per evaluation, improving the quality--compute trade-off without adding corresponding benchmark capability. The 128-NFE result cautions that this interpretation remains preliminary. Finally, EOS-padding supervision makes the fixed-width objective different from a pure answer-only likelihood and deserves explicit ablation.

Perplexity alone is insufficient for selecting a diffusion generator. Our soft-frontier masking ablation lowered Phi-4 generative perplexity but substantially reduced sliding model-token Distinct-1/2/3 and produced visibly more repetitive answers (Appendix~\ref{app:frontier}). DiffuLLaMA similarly pairs external-model perplexity with distinct bigrams to check that low perplexity was not obtained through repetition \citep{gong2025diffullama}. More fundamentally, external generative perplexity reflects a separate AR model's preferences and ignores diversity, so repeated high-probability text can score deceptively well \citep{turok2026duel}. Theory likewise distinguishes token-level perplexity from whole-sequence correctness: a masked diffusion model may approach favorable perplexity with few steps while still requiring a step count that scales with sequence length for low sequence error \citep{feng2025benefit}. We therefore triangulate external perplexity with Distinct-1/2/3, task accuracy, and generated samples. Distinct-$n$ is itself only a repetition diagnostic, so grammaticality, coherence, and actual usability ultimately require systematic qualitative inspection rather than replacement by any single automatic score.

The literature comparison sharpens the trade-off. DiffuLLaMA and Dream reuse AR initialization but update the full model for 65B and 580B tokens, respectively; Efficient-DLM invests 500B tokens and architectural training choices specifically designed to retain causal-model behavior and enable cached block decoding \citep{gong2025diffullama,ye2025dream,fu2025efficientdlm}. iLLaDA's strong reported results come from 12T-token diffusion pretraining plus repeated large-scale SFT, a fundamentally different compute regime \citep{nie2026illada}. Fast-dLLM v2 and SDLM show that retaining blockwise or sequential structure can reduce conversion cost while enabling practical caching and variable-length generation \citep{wu2025fastdllmv2,liu2025sdlm}; our complementary question is how far a fully bidirectional model can be converted with adapter-only updates. Uno is closer to our parameter-efficient philosophy because it also trains LoRA diffusion weights, but its goal is different: an AR verifier guarantees samples from the original AR distribution, whereas \method{} seeks an independently usable fully bidirectional generator \citep{sahoo2026uno}. Thus the higher published scores in Appendix~\ref{app:published-results} do not isolate a single superior masking objective; they bundle substantially different data budgets and modeling or decoding machinery.

The incremental scale is central to the intended contribution. One primary \method{} conversion processes orders of magnitude fewer token positions than the full-model conversion systems in Table~\ref{tab:compute}, while leaving most parent parameters frozen. This comparison does not erase the sunk cost of AR pretraining, nor prove a proportional reduction in energy or emissions. It does show that an existing foundation checkpoint can be repurposed without repeating that pretraining or reserving a large cluster. That distinction matters both economically and for widening participation in diffusion-model research; direct power and carbon measurements remain necessary for an environmental comparison \citep{strubell2019energy,patterson2021carbon}.

This is also why we do not claim that Appendix~\ref{app:published-results} is a leaderboard. Our direct LLaDA run holds prompts, subsets, extraction, and generation code fixed. The remaining columns are literature context, useful for locating the attainable performance range but unsuitable for causal comparison. A future study can add these systems to the shared harness one at a time, provided their task-specific inference procedures are faithfully reproduced---a requirement emphasized by the dLLM framework \citep{zhou2026dllm}.

\section{Limitations}

The current benchmark results use 50 examples per reported task and do not yet contain every Ministral evaluation. Unless expanded, these estimates have high sampling uncertainty. Only one training seed is available per model, so architecture and random-seed effects are not separated. The four backbones differ in tokenizer, pretraining data, alignment, attention implementation, and nominal parameter count. Although chosen as the latest comparable text-only releases when the study was designed, they are not guaranteed to remain the latest checkpoints at publication time. Phi-4 perplexity favors text that its own AR distribution considers probable, ignores diversity, and is not a diffusion likelihood. Distinct-$n$ detects local repetition but not factuality, grammar, or global coherence. Published LLaDA numbers are not directly comparable to our harness. We also have not yet established wall-clock speedups: $\mathrm{NFE}<L$ reduces sequential model calls but does not automatically imply lower latency because each call processes the full answer block. Finally, we did not instrument the runs for energy consumption or carbon intensity. Single-GPU execution and lower incremental training volume support accessibility and compute-efficiency claims, not a quantified sustainability claim.

\section{Conclusion}

\method{} provides a common, parameter-efficient route from instruction-tuned AR checkpoints to masked diffusion generators across Gemma, Llama, Qwen, and Ministral. The preliminary evidence shows broad transfer of the conversion mechanism, but also substantial dependence on the parent model, inference budget, and evaluation metric. By fitting every conversion on a single Google Colab GPU, the work offers a reproducible pathway for researchers who cannot retrain a foundation model or provision a large accelerator cluster. Continued training appears more promising for improving generation in the parallel decoding regime than for improving the aggregate downstream benchmark score, a hypothesis the final checkpoint sweep will test. Completing the paired AR evaluation will determine not merely whether these models can diffuse, but how much of their original capability survives conversion and whether that modest incremental cost is justified relative to purpose-trained diffusion models.

\section{Bidirectional infilling diagnostic}
\label{app:bidirectional-infilling}

This diagnostic is designed to isolate access to right-hand evidence, which ordinary left-to-right generation benchmarks do not test directly. It is synthetic and deterministic: no external corpus is downloaded, and no model is fine-tuned on the evaluation examples. The suite contains 100 examples divided evenly between two mechanically generated templates. In the \emph{identifier-copy} subset, a response first leaves an access code blank and a later verification sentence states the code. In the \emph{ordered-relation} subset, a response leaves the label immediately preceding an anchor blank and a later sentence gives the complete local order. Targets are generated alphanumeric identifiers or nonce labels; they are absent from the instruction and answer prefix and occur in the visible suffix. The two templates test the same right-context dependency through different surface forms. They should be interpreted as a controlled functional test, not as a broad benchmark of reasoning, factual knowledge, poetry completion, or free-form infilling quality.

For each model, the instruction is rendered with its native chat template. Let $l_k$ denote the tokenized chat context and answer prefix for example $k$, let $y_k=(y_k^1,\ldots,y_k^{m_k})$ be its target span, and let $r_k$ be the answer suffix. Target length $m_k$ is determined by the model's own tokenizer. Every target token is replaced simultaneously by the model's native mask token, producing the full-context input
\begin{equation}
    z_k^{\mathrm{full}} = [l_k,\underbrace{\mathtt{MASK},\ldots,\mathtt{MASK}}_{m_k},r_k].
\end{equation}
The converted model performs one bidirectional forward pass and predicts each target from the logits at the same position. There is no iterative sampler, denoising schedule, temperature, top-$k$ filter, or EOS heuristic in this test. Greedy predictions are used only to calculate exact match and token accuracy; target probabilities provide the likelihood diagnostics.

We evaluate two controls. First, the \emph{prefix-only} condition removes $r_k$ while retaining the same prompt, masked target, model weights, and scoring code. The difference between full-context and prefix-only performance therefore measures the benefit obtained from the right-hand evidence within the converted model. Second, when an unmerged PEFT checkpoint is available, we disable its adapter, restore the parent normalization state, reinstate causal attention, and score the clean target with shifted next-token logits. This AR control does not receive the suffix. It is teacher-forced, so target token $y_k^j$ for $j>1$ may condition on the preceding gold target tokens $y_k^{<j}$; this makes the comparison favorable to the causal parent while preserving its structural inability to condition on $r_k$. A merged checkpoint cannot recover its untouched parent internally, so its AR control must come from the corresponding unmerged run and is deduplicated when several adapters share that parent.

For condition $c$, exact match and token accuracy are
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{EM}_c &= \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
    \mathbb{1}\!\left[\hat{y}_{k,c}=y_k\right], \\
    \mathrm{TokAcc}_c &=
    \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{m_k}
    \mathbb{1}\!\left[\hat{y}_{k,c}^{j}=y_k^j\right]}
    {\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_k},
\end{align}
where $K=100$, $m_k$ is the target length in model tokens, $y_k$ is the gold target, and $\hat{y}_{k,c}$ is the condition's position-wise argmax prediction. We also report the token-weighted negative log-likelihood and perplexity,
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{NLL}_c &= -\frac{1}{\sum_k m_k}
    \sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{m_k}
    \log p_c(y_k^j), \\
    \mathrm{PPL}_c &= \exp(\mathrm{NLL}_c).
\end{align}
Suffix gains are $\Delta\mathrm{EM}=\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{full}}-\mathrm{EM}_{\mathrm{prefix}}$ and $\Delta\mathrm{TokAcc}=\mathrm{TokAcc}_{\mathrm{full}}-\mathrm{TokAcc}_{\mathrm{prefix}}$; the likelihood gain is $\mathrm{NLL}_{\mathrm{prefix}}-\mathrm{NLL}_{\mathrm{full}}$, so a positive value always denotes useful right context. Aggregate and per-template summaries are retained, together with every tokenized target, prediction, and per-example loss, in the validation artifacts.

\section{Cost and operational-emissions estimation}
\label{app:cost}

Table~\ref{tab:compute} compares the cost of training a model that produces diffusion behavior. It should thus be taken into account that some of these models build on an AR parent, whereas others are trained from scratch. It also excludes exploratory runs, failed jobs, dataset construction, evaluation, storage, and inference. The estimates must therefore not be interpreted as total project or lifecycle footprints.

For BYOD we use the observed 3.2 GPU-hours for the conversion of a Llama 3.1 8B model for diffusive generation. For a prior work that reports GPU-hours, we retain that value. Where a paper reports model size but not time, we approximate training compute as $C\approx6ND$ FLOPs \citep{hoffmann2022chinchilla}, where $C$ is total training compute, $N$ is the number of model parameters, and $D$ is the number of processed training-token positions. We then calculate
\begin{equation}
G_{\mathrm{est}}=\frac{6ND}{0.40\,(989\times10^{12})\,3600},
\end{equation}
where $G_{\mathrm{est}}$ is aggregate estimated GPU-hours, 0.40 is the assumed model FLOPs utilization (MFU), $989\times10^{12}$ is the NVIDIA H100's dense BF16 Tensor Core throughput in FLOP/s \citep{nvidiaH100}, and 3600 converts seconds to hours. The 40\% MFU assumption is consistent with the 39.0--45.0\% BF16 MFUs reported by FP8-LM for GPT-style 7B, 13B, and 175B training on H100 GPUs \citep{peng2023fp8lm}. Actual utilization varies considerably with architecture, sequence length, parallelization, communication, activation recomputation, and implementation efficiency. The assumption is therefore a standardized order-of-magnitude convention rather than a reconstruction of any authors' cluster. It yields the daggered values in Table~\ref{tab:compute}. The Dream and iLLaDA estimates cover their main 580B- and 12T-token stages but omit the smaller instruction-tuning additions, so they are lower bounds.

Table~\ref{tab:compute-calibration} calibrates the same calculation against studies that report both a usable token count and elapsed accelerator-hours \citep{nie2025llada,wu2025fastdllmv2}. For LLaDA, it predicts approximately 77,519 GPU-hours, compared with 130,000 reported H800 GPU-hours, corresponding to an implied utilization of 23.9\%. Thus 40\% can be optimistic about achieved utilization and correspondingly conservative with respect to estimated accelerator-hours, rental cost, and emissions. Fast-dLLM v2 displays a still larger gap. Its specialized training concatenates clean and noised views and uses a nonstandard attention pattern, illustrating that an ``implied MFU'' from $6ND$ is a calibration diagnostic, not a measured hardware-utilization statistic.

We do not apply this estimator to SDLM. Its released 3B training script launches 128 GPU workers, but neither accelerator type nor elapsed training time is reported \citep{liu2025sdlm}. More importantly, SDLM's 2.3B figure describes source-corpus tokens: its parallel-block construction rearranges each example and may append multiple noise/target blocks within a 5,632-token training sequence. Consequently, substituting 2.3B directly for $D$ would undercount the token positions actually processed, while the $6ND$ approximation would also omit the material sequence-length-dependent attention cost. We therefore report its known GPU count but leave GPU-hours, cost, and emissions as NR rather than present a misleading lower-bound estimate.

We convert BYOD time to a rental-equivalent cost using the September 2026 AWS US-East on-demand price of \$3.36 per hour for a one-GPU \texttt{g7e.2xlarge}, which contains the same 96-GB NVIDIA RTX PRO Server 6000 accelerator used in our Colab run \citep{awsG7e2026}. For other systems we use one consistent current major-cloud proxy: \$5.191 per accelerator-hour for an AWS \texttt{p5.4xlarge} H100 Capacity Block in US East \citep{awsCapacity2026}. Thus these figures answer ``what would the stated or estimated accelerator-hours cost at a public contemporary rate?'', not what the original authors paid. They exclude storage, egress, idle reservation time, taxes, and discounts.

Operational emissions are estimated as energy use multiplied by the data center's power usage effectiveness (PUE) and the grid carbon intensity \citep{lannelongue2021green,patterson2021carbon}:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{E}_{\mathrm{CO_2e}}=G\,P_{\mathrm{GPU}}\,\mathrm{PUE}\,I_{\mathrm{grid}}.
\end{equation}
Here $\widehat{E}_{\mathrm{CO_2e}}$ is estimated operational emissions in kg CO$_2$e, $G$ is aggregate GPU-hours, $P_{\mathrm{GPU}}$ is assumed per-GPU power draw in kW, PUE is the dimensionless data-center power usage effectiveness, and $I_{\mathrm{grid}}$ is grid carbon intensity in kg CO$_2$e/kWh. We use 0.60 kW for RTX PRO 6000 runs, matching NVIDIA's stated maximum board power \citep{nvidiaRTXPro6000}, and a 0.70-kW H100-class proxy for all other entries except the reported A100 run, for which we use 0.40 kW \citep{nvidiaH100,nvidiaA100}. We assume PUE 1.09, Google's reported fleet-wide value \citep{googlePUE2025}, and the IEA's 2024 global-average generation intensity of 0.445 kg CO$_2$e/kWh \citep{ieaElectricity2025}. For example, one BYOD stage gives $3.2\times0.60\times1.09\times0.445=0.93$ kg CO$_2$e. These are location-independent GPU-only operating scenarios: they assume full rated GPU power, omit host CPU/memory/network energy and embodied hardware emissions, and do not account for renewable-energy procurement. Direct power telemetry and the datacenter's time- and location-specific marginal carbon intensity would be required for a measured footprint.

Merging is an algebraic reparameterization rather than an additional training stage: each base projection $W$ is replaced by $W+(\alpha/r)BA$, where $A$ and $B$ are the trained rank-$r$ LoRA matrices and $\alpha$ is their scaling parameter. \section{Frontier-masking ablation}
\label{app:frontier}

The selected main runs use the simpler IID corruption process: after sampling the example-level noise fraction $t$, each eligible answer or EOS-padding position is independently masked with probability $t$. In a later ablation, we tested \emph{soft-frontier masking}, intended to resemble partially completed left-to-right text while retaining stochastic exceptions. For a frontier example, masking probability increases sigmoidally with answer position around a frontier determined by $t$; the tested configuration used a 0.75 probability of selecting this branch per example, $\epsilon=0.03$, and $\tau=3.0$, with the remaining examples using IID corruption. Token losses use the corresponding inverse masking-probability correction.

The frontier variant achieved lower external Phi-4 generative perplexity than the matched IID variant, but substantially lower sliding model-token Distinct-1, Distinct-2, and Distinct-3, accompanied by visibly more immediate token and phrase repetition.
