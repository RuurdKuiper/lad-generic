\begin{abstract}
Diffusion language models (DLMs) permit parallel token updates and flexible generation orders, but leading systems rely on costly pretraining or continued full-model training. We ask how efficiently diffusion generation can instead be induced in existing instruction-tuned autoregressive models. Build Your Own DLM (BYOD) replaces causal attention with bidirectional attention and trains only query/value LoRA adapters under a masked denoising objective, leaving 94.2--95.9\% of parameters frozen. Without architecture-specific tuning, the same recipe converts Gemma 2 9B, Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, and Ministral 8B, with each 25,000-update conversion processing at most 102.4M token positions in 3.1--4.0 hours on one Google Colab GPU. All four models generate from fully masked sequences, use right context in a controlled attention test, and retain substantial parent-model knowledge. BYOD-Gemma is the strongest conversion on our nine-task evaluation, reaching 53.0\% average accuracy compared with 51.9\% for LLaDA 8B Instruct in the same harness. Open-generation quality remains the principal limitation: at 64 denoising network evaluations (NFE), converted-model perplexity is 8.3--11.1 versus 4.1 for LLaDA and 2.2--3.0 for the AR parents, with generally lower Distinct-$n$ scores as well. BYOD therefore establishes a practical, reproducible route for broad experimentation with diffusion generation while identifying the quality gap that efficient conversion must next close.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Autoregressive (AR) language models generate a sequence from left to right. This method has proven extremely effective due to its simple learning target and effective scaling capabilities. However, it also has trade-offs: it fixes the generation order, limits the model to unidirectional attention, and predicts only one new token at a time. Diffusion language models (DLMs) instead begin with a corrupted sequence and can iteratively reconstruct multiple positions at a time using bidirectional context \citep{austin2021structured,sahoo2024simple,shi2024simplified}. Their number of function evaluations (NFE) can be chosen independently of output length, and early low-probability predictions can be revised. However, training or adapting models for diffusive generation has so far been computationally intensive and therefore expensive.

Our primary aim is therefore to devise a recipe optimized foremost for compute efficiency and reproducibility when converting AR models into DLMs, rather than to pretrain a new diffusion foundation model for every architecture. Efficiency matters for three connected reasons. First, full diffusion pretraining is financially and environmentally costly: under our standardized rental and electricity scenario, the reported LLaDA run corresponds to approximately \$675,000 and 44,100 kg CO$_2$e (Table~\ref{tab:compute}). These are not the authors' bill or measured emissions---operational carbon depends strongly on utilization, datacenter efficiency, location, and energy supply---but they illustrate the scale of the barrier \citep{strubell2019energy,patterson2021carbon}. Second, a conversion that completes in hours on one widely available cloud instance makes hands-on DLM research accessible beyond laboratories with multi-node clusters. Third, applying the same inexpensive intervention to multiple existing model families makes it feasible to study how architecture and parent-model choice affect diffusion generation without repeating foundation-model pretraining for every comparison.

We study this question with \method{}, a generalizable conversion and evaluation pipeline for four instruction-tuned decoder families: Gemma~2~9B, Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen2.5~7B, and Ministral~8B. We selected the latest text-only instruction checkpoint from each family with a comparable parameter count. Multimodal variants are not used to avoid modality-specific training as an additional confounder. Every backbone receives the same bidirectional attention intervention, LoRA target modules, and masked-diffusion objective. This paired design separates conversion quality from the strength of the starting checkpoint. We directly compare our models against LLaDA~8B Instruct because its standard masked-diffusion objective, instruction-tuning setup, and overlapping benchmark suite make it the cleanest external control. We additionally report published DiffuLLaMA, Dream-Instruct, iLLaDA-Instruct, and Efficient-DLM values, but keep them protocol-separated: their additional attention, masking, scheduling, data, and inference techniques would confound a controlled comparison.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a compute-efficient recipe that converts four instruction-tuned AR model families to masked diffusion using only a few single-GPU hours and LoRA parameter updates;
    \item a paired evaluation of each diffusion model and its frozen AR parent across general knowledge, reasoning, mathematics, and code benchmarks;
    \item a controlled analysis of training duration, denoising-step count, validation loss, perplexity and distinct n-grams; and
    \item publicly available training and inference code directly available through Google Colab and Github, and interactive demos for all four models, making DLM conversion and qualitative evaluation easily accessible.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

Discrete diffusion models corrupt and reconstruct categorical sequences rather than adding Gaussian noise. D3PM formalized categorical corruption with transition matrices, including an absorbing process in which tokens transition to a dedicated mask state \citep{austin2021structured}. MDLM and contemporaneous work showed that this masked formulation admits a simple noise-weighted cross-entropy objective, while RADD reparameterized the predictor in terms of conditional distributions over clean tokens \citep{sahoo2024simple,shi2024simplified,ou2024absorbing}. These approaches form the basis of masked diffusion language models (MDLMs). Uniform (or multinomial) diffusion, which instead corrupts tokens with samples from the ordinary vocabulary, is another option \citep{hoogeboom2021multinomial}; we use the simpler absorbing-mask denoising objective employed by LLaDA.

The authors of LLaDA were the first to show that masked diffusion can scale to an 8B-parameter general-purpose language model \citep{nie2025llada}. Although its architecture follows Llama~3.1~8B, LLaDA was pre-trained from scratch using 2.3T tokens and 130,000 GPU-hours. AR-to-diffusion conversion instead amortizes knowledge already acquired by an AR checkpoint. DiffuLLaMA established this route through 65B tokens of full-model continued training at 7B scale \citep{gong2025diffullama}. Dream adopts DiffuLLaMA's shifted-prediction initialization, continues Qwen2.5 for 580B tokens, and adds context-adaptive token-level noise rescheduling \citep{ye2025dream}. Efficient-DLM combines 500B tokens of continued training with blockwise attention and position-dependent masking \citep{fu2025efficientdlm}, whereas iLLaDA returns to from-scratch diffusion pretraining at substantially larger scale \citep{nie2026illada}. Diffusion conversion has also been demonstrated at larger sparse scales by RND1 (30B parameters), LLaDA2.0 (16B and 100B), and DiffusionGemma (25.2B, using uniform diffusion) \citep{chandrasegaran2025rnd1,bie2025llada20,diffusiongemma2026}.

Hybrid and blockwise models offer a different compromise between bidirectionality and deployability. Block Diffusion is autoregressive across blocks but diffusive within each block, enabling variable-length generation and inter-block KV caching \citep{arriola2025block}. Fast-dLLM v2 converts Qwen2.5 models using full-parameter fine-tuning with approximately 1B tokens and combines blockwise complementary masking with hierarchical caching \citep{wu2025fastdllmv2}. These systems retain more left-to-right structure than our fully bidirectional answer block, making them especially relevant to speed, caching, and arbitrary-length generation but not suited as direct controls for our intervention.

LAD first showed that LoRA can induce diffusion generation, using a hybrid objective that combines masking with token swaps, duplications, and span shifts \citep{kuiper2025lad,hu2022lora}. \method{} instead tests whether a simpler IID absorbing-mask objective can transfer reliably across model families. Other LoRA-based diffusion work addresses different goals: Uno learns diffusion-draft adapters while retaining the AR model as verifier, the dLLM framework uses LoRA to fine-tune already-diffusive checkpoints rather than for its Tiny-A2D conversion, and NaRA adapts existing diffusion models with noise-conditioned LoRA \citep{sahoo2026uno,zhou2026dllm,wang2026nara}. Thus, their LoRA components augment already diffusively generating models, rather than converting AR model families into standalone diffusion models.

Table~\ref{tab:compute} places (estimates of) the training budgets of models of comparable size to our intended conversion targets side by side. For AR-to-diffusion conversion methods, these budgets exclude the substantial earlier cost of training the AR parent; for from-scratch methods they include diffusion pretraining. Dollar and operational-emissions columns are rental-equivalent estimates under one documented pricing and energy scenario, not the authors' actual bills or measured footprints; Appendix~\ref{app:cost} gives the complete calculation and its limitations.

Our study has explicitly chosen to hold the adaptation mechanism as constant as the architectures permit, repeat it across four families, and benchmark each converted model against the exact AR checkpoint from which it originated. This paired comparison is essential because it allows us to compare our results directly with the prior capability of the parent model.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Masked-diffusion training objective}

The training objective teaches the adapted model to reconstruct masked response tokens while conditioning on an uncorrupted instruction.

Let $p$ be the instruction, $x_0=(x_0^1,\ldots,x_0^L)$ the clean response region, $A$ the answer positions, and $P$ the EOS-padding positions. For each example, we sample $t\sim\mathcal{U}[10^{-3},1]$ and independently mask each position in $A\cup P$ with probability $t$, yielding the corrupted sequence $x_t$ and masked set $M_t$. For either region $R\in\{A,P\}$, we calculate the same-position reconstruction loss
\begin{equation}
\ell_R(\theta)=-\frac{1}{t|R|}
\sum_{i\in M_t\cap R}
\log p_{\phi,\theta}(x_0^i\mid p,x_t).
\label{eq:region-loss}
\end{equation}
We then combine the answer and padding losses:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,x_t}
\left[0.9\ell_A(\theta)+0.1\ell_P(\theta)\right].
    \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
Here, $p_{\phi,\theta}$ is the conditional token distribution, $\phi$ denotes the frozen parent parameters, and $\theta$ the LoRA parameters. The first terminating EOS is included as a genuine answer token; all subsequent EOS tokens are treated as padding. The factor $1/t$ corrects for the sampled masking rate. Prompt and chat-template tokens remain visible to the model and are not part of the loss. Training masks are resampled online; validation masks are fixed by example index and seed.

\subsection{Autoregressive-to-diffusion conversion}

To convert an autoregressive model to a diffusion-style model, we replace the causal mask in every layer with a bidirectional attention mask, so every prompt, answer, and EOS-padding position can attend to every position. We attach LoRA adapters to every self-attention query and value projection, with rank $r=1024$, scaling $\alpha=1024$, and dropout zero. The rest of the model architecture and output vocabulary remain unchanged. Backbone weights, token embeddings, the language-model head, biases, and normalization layers are frozen. The pretrained output head is also retained unchanged: whereas autoregressive training compares each position’s logits with the next token through shifted labels, BYOD removes this label shift and predicts the clean token at the same position from the bidirectionally attended, corrupted sequence. Each model reuses an existing single-token string from its tokenizer (\texttt{MASK} for Gemma, Llama, and Qwen; \texttt{<?>} for Ministral), avoiding vocabulary expansion.

We fix the LoRA rank across model families to retain one architecture-independent recipe. Matching trainable parameter counts would require model-specific ranks and would still not control for differences in depth, width, and attention design. This results in variation in trainable parameters, which is therefore a potential confounder in cross-family comparisons. Every conversion was trained on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU in Google Colab, without distributed parallelism; Table~\ref{tab:models} reports parameter counts and conversion times.

For deployment, each adapter can be folded into its parent projection as $W_{\mathrm{merged}}=W+(\alpha/r)BA$, where $W$ is the frozen projection and $A$ and $B$ are the learned low-rank matrices. This removes the adapter branch while retaining the bidirectional attention intervention. Appendix~\ref{app:merge} evaluates whether merging changes model behavior.

\subsection{Training data}

We construct a one-million-example mixture containing 45\% general instruction following, 18\% multiple-choice reasoning, 18\% mathematics, and 19\% code. Within these groups, the source weights are: Clean-Instruct-3M/T\"ulu~3/Alpaca-GPT4/Alpaca at 40/35/20/5 \citep{cleaninstruct3m,lambert2024tulu3,peng2023instructiongpt4,taori2023alpaca}; MMLU/HellaSwag/ARC-Easy at 73/25/2 \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu,zellers2019hellaswag,clark2018arc}; Orca-Math/GSM8K at 95/5 \citep{mitra2024orcamath,cobbe2021gsm8k}; and OpenCoder/MBPP at 99.7/0.3 \citep{huang2024opencoder,austin2021mbpp}. When applicable, only the training splits are used. We deduplicate normalized prompts, exclude exact matches with held-out benchmark prompts, and retain only examples that fit the 256-token training length. Each model receives the same shuffled examples, retokenized with its native chat template and tokenizer. Prompt tokens remain clean; only answer and EOS-padding positions can be corrupted.

The four primary models see the same dataset prefix. BYOD-Llama-50k uses the same fixed shuffle for a longer, 800,000-example run with newly sampled corruptions. Exact run sizes and optimization settings are given in Appendix~\ref{app:training-settings}.

\subsection{Inference}
Inference begins from a fixed-length, fully masked answer block. At each denoising step, the model predicts all masked positions, retains the most confident predictions, and remasks lower-confidence positions; accepted tokens remain unmasked. This can resolve several output tokens per network evaluation. Task-specific lengths, denoising budgets, temperatures, and EOS/EOT handling are given in Appendix~\ref{app:inference-settings}.

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Paired models and baselines}
We refer to the four conversions as BYOD-Gemma, BYOD-Qwen, BYOD-Ministral, and BYOD-Llama. For Llama, we distinguish the primary and longer-trained checkpoints as BYOD-Llama-25k and BYOD-Llama-50k. We compare each model with its unchanged AR parent. LLaDA~8B Instruct serves as an external diffusion baseline in the same harness; results reported by other papers are presented separately because their protocols differ. We use Llama for the longer run and checkpoint-wise analysis because DiffuLLaMA, LLaDA, and iLLaDA use Llama or closely related Llama-style architectures, making it the clearest basis for comparison with prominent general-purpose DLMs \citep{gong2025diffullama,nie2025llada,nie2026illada}. We also compare BYOD-Llama-25k before and after folding its LoRA weights into the base model. The nine standard task benchmarks use greedy token selection and low-confidence remasking, while open-ended generation uses temperature 0.7 and delayed EOS/EOT acceptance. Complete settings are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:inference-settings}.

\subsection{Task benchmarks}
Our benchmark set contains the validation/test sets of MMLU, MMLU-Pro, HellaSwag, ARC-Challenge, GSM8K, MATH-500, GPQA-Main, HumanEval, and MBPP \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu,wang2024mmlupro,zellers2019hellaswag,clark2018arc,cobbe2021gsm8k,hendrycks2021math,rein2023gpqa,chen2021codex,austin2021mbpp}. We score multiple-choice tasks by option label, GSM8K by its final number, MATH by its normalized final boxed expression, and code tasks by whether the generated program passes the supplied tests in an isolated subprocess.

\subsection{Open-ended generation}
We assess open-ended generation with perplexity and Distinct-1/2/3. Standard masked diffusion does not directly provide the left-to-right sequence likelihood used for AR perplexity, and its usual evidence lower bound is not a proper test-time likelihood \citep{turok2026duel}. We therefore follow prior DLM work and use frozen Microsoft Phi-4 14B as an external perplexity scorer \citep{gong2025diffullama}. We pool negative log-likelihoods across all non-padding tokens of all generated answers before exponentiating, so every token has equal weight. Because this score does not measure diversity, we also report sliding model-token Distinct-1/2/3 percentages after removing special tokens. Higher Distinct-$n$ indicates less local repetition \citep{li2016diversity}; in our experiments, it exposed cases where perplexity improved because output became more repetitive rather than better.

\subsection{Bidirectional attention test}
We test whether the converted diffusion models allow bidirectional information flow with a simple right-context copy task (Appendix~\ref{app:bidirectional-infilling}). Most of the response is pre-filled, and the model must fill an earlier masked position by copying a word shown later in the response. A causal model cannot change its prediction at an earlier position based on a later token, so sensitivity to this clue directly tests functional bidirectional attention. Figure~\ref{fig:bidirectional-example} shows one test item and its expected reconstruction.

\label{sec:bidirectional-infilling}

The bidirectional attention test showed that all models use right-context information. \section{Discussion}

The central result is that high-rank attention-only LoRA can convert four causal instruction models into bidirectional masked generators in a few hours on one GPU. The converted models do not surpass their AR parents on average, but LLaDA also trails the Llama AR baseline in our matched harness. BYOD nevertheless approaches LLaDA on benchmark accuracy, with BYOD-Gemma slightly exceeding it. The largest deficits relative to the AR parents occur in mathematics and code, where exact-match and execution-based scoring make small syntactic errors---which we frequently observe in diffusion outputs---complete failures. Conversion quality also depends on the backbone: Gemma performs best, while Qwen's stronger AR parent does not produce the strongest conversion. Parent quality, architecture, model size, and absolute adapter size remain confounded.

The single-Colab workflow broadens access to DLM experimentation, although it is not cost-free. The public demos also make grammaticality, coherence, and denoising failures easier to inspect than aggregate metrics alone; representative trajectories appear in Appendix~\ref{app:demo-trajectories}. Their EOS-aware early stopping can finish before the fixed denoising budget when the pre-EOS answer stabilizes, following related work on variable-length and token-level stopping \citep{yang2025dllmvar,liu2026voidpadding,wang2026audioconditioned,kohut2026just}.

Our LoRA rank of 1024 is relatively high for task adaptation, but we hypothesized that learning a new generation process requires a large change; an earlier study into diffusion adaptation also found rank 1024 preferable to smaller ranks, but found no consistent gains at rank 2048 for similar models \citep{kuiper2025lad}. During the AR-to-DLM conversion, all original parameters remains frozen, preventing direct overwriting of pretrained weights and potentially reducing catastrophic forgetting \citep{luo2023catastrophic}. Once diffusion generation has been established, the adapters can be merged into the base projections, restoring the original model size while retaining broadly similar behavior (Appendix~\ref{app:merge}). Future work should study whether the merged diffusion model could then initialize partial or full-model fine-tuning for diffusion generation. Because the new generation paradigm is learned before the original weights are exposed to optimization, this staged approach may require less disruptive updates and therefore reduce catastrophic forgetting.

The main remaining weakness is linguistic fluency. Perplexity and Distinct-1/2/3 trail both the AR parents and LLaDA, especially when NFE is below output length, and this is also noticeable qualitatively when using the models through the demo interface. While validation loss still decreases almost monotonically even during the longer training run, longer training alone may be insufficient; useful ablations would include larger effective batches, alternative denoising targets, EOS-padding supervision, and adapters in key/output projections, MLPs, or normalization layers.

We found that perplexity alone is not sufficient to measure linguistic fluency. An external AR scorer can favor repetitive high-probability text, while Distinct-$n$ captures repetition but not factuality, grammar, or coherence \citep{gong2025diffullama,turok2026duel}. We therefore combine perplexity, Distinct-1/2/3, task accuracy, and include demos for qualitative inspection of generated samples, rather than treating any one metric as definitive.

Most large DLMs have differences in training objectives, evaluation datasets or decoding algorithms, so their published scores are not controlled comparisons. For this reason, we evaluated the simplest and cleanest comparable model, LLaDA, within our own evaluation harness, and used this as our primary external baseline; the remaining literature values in Appendix~\ref{app:published-results} provide context only. Table~\ref{tab:compute} shows the scale difference, but its cost and emissions estimates do not replace measured power or location-specific carbon accounting.

\section{Limitations}
The main limitation is that efficient conversion does not yet produce generation quality on par with the AR parents or LLaDA, especially when NFE is below output length. We also measure inference budget in network evaluations rather than wall-clock latency. Fewer sequential evaluations do not guarantee a speedup because each diffusion step processes the full answer block. Our efficiency claim therefore concerns training the conversion, not measured inference acceleration.

The cross-backbone comparison is not a controlled architecture study. The parents differ in size, tokenizer, pretraining, alignment, and attention design. Fixing LoRA rank also gives each backbone a different absolute adapter budget. Gemma's leading result therefore cannot be attributed separately to architecture, parent quality, model size, or trainable parameter count.

Finally, the automatic generation metrics cover only part of text quality. Phi-4 perplexity reflects the preferences of an external AR model, while Distinct-$n$ detects local repetition but not factuality, grammar, or coherence. Published results from other DLMs use different protocols; only LLaDA was rerun in our harness. We also did not measure energy use or carbon intensity directly, so the resource analysis supports relative scale and accessibility claims rather than a measured environmental footprint.

\section{Conclusion}

\method{} provides a common, efficient route from instruction-tuned AR checkpoints to masked diffusion generators across Gemma, Llama, Qwen, and Ministral. The experiments show that diffusive generation with bidirectional attention can be obtained in a few hours on one Google Colab GPU, while retaining substantial parent-model knowledge. However, fluency and diversity still lag behind both their AR parents and other large DLMs, especially when NFE is below output length. A promising next step is to use the merged BYOD model to initialize further partial or full diffusion fine-tuning, testing whether generation quality can improve while retaining the parent's capabilities. In its present form, BYOD enables rapid, controlled diffusion experiments across a range of established model families using minimal compute infrastructure.

\section{Cost and operational-emissions estimation}
\label{app:cost}

Table~\ref{tab:compute} compares the cost of training a model that produces diffusion behavior. It should thus be taken into account that some of these models build on an AR parent, whereas others are trained from scratch. It also excludes exploratory runs, failed jobs, dataset construction, evaluation, storage, and inference. The estimates must therefore not be interpreted as total project or lifecycle footprints.

For BYOD we use the observed 3.2 GPU-hours for the conversion of a Llama 3.1 8B model for diffusive generation. For a prior work that reports GPU-hours, we retain that value. Where a paper reports model size but not time, we approximate training compute as $C\approx6ND$ FLOPs \citep{hoffmann2022chinchilla}, where $C$ is total training compute, $N$ is the number of model parameters, and $D$ is the number of processed training-token positions. We then calculate
\begin{equation}
G_{\mathrm{est}}=\frac{6ND}{0.40\,(989\times10^{12})\,3600},
\end{equation}
where $G_{\mathrm{est}}$ is aggregate estimated GPU-hours, 0.40 is the assumed model FLOPs utilization (MFU), $989\times10^{12}$ is the NVIDIA H100's dense BF16 Tensor Core throughput in FLOP/s \citep{nvidiaH100}, and 3600 converts seconds to hours. The 40\% MFU assumption is consistent with the 39.0--45.0\% BF16 MFUs reported by FP8-LM for GPT-style 7B, 13B, and 175B training on H100 GPUs \citep{peng2023fp8lm}. Actual utilization varies considerably with architecture, sequence length, parallelization, communication, activation recomputation, and implementation efficiency. The assumption is therefore a standardized order-of-magnitude convention rather than a reconstruction of any authors' cluster. It yields the daggered values in Table~\ref{tab:compute}. The Dream and iLLaDA estimates cover their main 580B- and 12T-token stages but omit the smaller instruction-tuning additions, so they are lower bounds.

Table~\ref{tab:compute-calibration} calibrates the same calculation against studies that report both a usable token count and elapsed accelerator-hours \citep{nie2025llada,wu2025fastdllmv2}. For LLaDA, it predicts approximately 77,519 GPU-hours, compared with 130,000 reported H800 GPU-hours, corresponding to an implied utilization of 23.9\%. Thus 40\% can be optimistic about achieved utilization and correspondingly conservative with respect to estimated accelerator-hours, rental cost, and emissions. Fast-dLLM v2 displays a still larger gap. Its specialized training concatenates clean and noised views and uses a nonstandard attention pattern, illustrating that an ``implied MFU'' from $6ND$ is a calibration diagnostic, not a measured hardware-utilization statistic.

We convert BYOD time to a rental-equivalent cost using the September 2026 AWS US-East on-demand price of \$3.36 per hour for a one-GPU \texttt{g7e.2xlarge}, which contains the same 96-GB NVIDIA RTX PRO Server 6000 accelerator used in our Colab run \citep{awsG7e2026}. For other systems we use one consistent current major-cloud proxy: \$5.191 per accelerator-hour for an AWS \texttt{p5.4xlarge} H100 Capacity Block in US East \citep{awsCapacity2026}. Thus these figures answer ``what would the stated or estimated accelerator-hours cost at a public contemporary rate?'', not what the original authors paid. They exclude storage, egress, idle reservation time, taxes, and discounts.

Operational emissions are estimated as energy use multiplied by the data center's power usage effectiveness (PUE) and the grid carbon intensity \citep{lannelongue2021green,patterson2021carbon}:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{E}_{\mathrm{CO_2e}}=G\,P_{\mathrm{GPU}}\,\mathrm{PUE}\,I_{\mathrm{grid}}.
\end{equation}
Here $\widehat{E}_{\mathrm{CO_2e}}$ is estimated operational emissions in kg CO$_2$e, $G$ is aggregate GPU-hours, $P_{\mathrm{GPU}}$ is assumed per-GPU power draw in kW, PUE is the dimensionless data-center power usage effectiveness, and $I_{\mathrm{grid}}$ is grid carbon intensity in kg CO$_2$e/kWh. We use 0.60 kW for RTX PRO 6000 runs, matching NVIDIA's stated maximum board power \citep{nvidiaRTXPro6000}, and a 0.70-kW H100-class proxy for all other entries except the reported A100 run, for which we use 0.40 kW \citep{nvidiaH100,nvidiaA100}. We assume PUE 1.09, Google's reported fleet-wide value \citep{googlePUE2025}, and the IEA's 2024 global-average generation intensity of 0.445 kg CO$_2$e/kWh \citep{ieaElectricity2025}. For example, one BYOD stage gives $3.2\times0.60\times1.09\times0.445=0.93$ kg CO$_2$e. These are location-independent GPU-only operating scenarios: they assume full rated GPU power, omit host CPU/memory/network energy and embodied hardware emissions, and do not account for renewable-energy procurement. Direct power telemetry and the datacenter's time- and location-specific marginal carbon intensity would be required for a measured footprint.

Merging is an algebraic reparameterization rather than an additional training stage: each base projection $W$ is replaced by $W+(\alpha/r)BA$, where $A$ and $B$ are the trained rank-$r$ LoRA matrices and $\alpha$ is their scaling parameter. Subject to floating-point rounding, evaluating the folded projection is equivalent to evaluating the base and LoRA branches separately. Open-generation metrics are slightly better after merging, while factual results vary modestly by task and the macro mean decreases by only 0.4 points. These reruns therefore support preservation of overall behavior, but are not precise enough to attribute task-level changes to merging; a logit-level deterministic parity test would isolate numerical merge error from generation and benchmark variance.

\section{Bidirectional attention test}
\label{app:bidirectional-infilling}

This deterministic test uses ordinary single-token target words. Each partial response has the form ``Answer:[MASK]. COPY: apple.'', with a different target in each example. The target is absent from the instruction and left context and appears explicitly after the mask. The converted model predicts the masked position by greedy argmax in one forward pass; no iterative sampling or generation settings are involved.

We score the same masked position twice: once with the later copy clue and once after deleting that clue. We report exact match for both conditions, their difference, and whether adding the clue changes the greedy prediction at all. Under causal attention, changing or deleting tokens to the right cannot affect logits at an earlier position, so both the exact-match gain and prediction-change rate must be zero. The latter separates use of right context from the stricter ability to follow the exact-copy instruction. This is a functional attention check, not a benchmark of reasoning or general infilling quality.
